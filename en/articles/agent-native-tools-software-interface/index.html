Will Agent-Native Tools Push Software Interfaces into the Background?

From trying out a native Agent video editing tool, rethinking the division of labor between humans and software: humans define tasks and verify results, while the Agent handles the execution details.

Published 2026-07-12 | Last Updated 2026-07-12

When AI can already receive tasks, arrange processing steps, and return results for review, many software interfaces may shift from where humans operate to where the Agent works in the background.

Who is this for
  • People who are learning AI and want to hand over the entire workflow to an Agent
  • People thinking about how to design AI applications
What you can take away
  • A framework that treats AI as an outsourced team
  • A perspective to judge whether a tool is truly native to an Agent

My first impression was: the interface may move to the background

Recently, I saw a native Agent video editing tool called ChatCut, which I am currently trying out. My first impression was that many software interfaces may no longer need to be placed at the front for human operation.

Previously, video editing software was needed because humans had to manually select clips, adjust timelines, add subtitles, and edit music. However, if the video can be handled by AI, the tasks I need to assign will decrease: what I need to film, how much budget I am willing to provide, and the quality standards for the final product. Here, the budget also includes how many tokens and how much time I am willing to spend.

The tools remain, but the responsibilities change Previously, humans operated tools to complete tasks. In the future, it may be the Agent operating tools to complete tasks, while humans define tasks and verify results.

This is actually similar to hiring an outsourced team

Assume I hire a photography team to shoot a video. I don't need to ask them about the camera model, which computer they use, or which editing software they use. What I need to confirm is whether they know what I need to film, the budget, and when they can deliver the final product.

After delivery, if I think a certain part needs editing, I can clearly state in LINE where the changes are needed. The team will make the changes and send me a new version. Throughout the process, I care about the requirements, communication, and quality, not about which button they pressed internally.

How can I now delegate work to AI?

I will clearly explain three things:

  1. Task: What needs to be completed this time.
  2. Budget: How much time, how many tokens, and how much generation quota I am willing to spend.
  3. Acceptance Criteria: What the final result should look like, and which parts cannot be wrong.

After clearly explaining these three things, AI will have space to arrange the work on its own. Humans do not need to monitor every step. The final review and decision to approve will still be retained.

Why I think we can now start thinking this way

I have recently used Fable for almost a week, and yesterday I used ChatGPT 5.6 Sol extensively. Based on my own experience, many AI models can now independently complete a segment of work.

Previously, we always monitored AI because it often misunderstood, missed steps, or deviated halfway. Although errors still occur, the review and acceptance process has not disappeared. However, some tasks can now be let the AI handle on its own, reaching a certain level before coming back to review.

This is my personal experience Model capabilities and available quotas vary depending on the plan, time, and type of task. What I am saying is based on my own experience during this period, not all accounts will be the same.

Agent-native tools are built to solve tasks, not just expose features

I'm still learning my way around ChatCut, so this article is not recommending a specific set of tools. What I want to share is a mindset: if you are learning AI, start by treating the agent as an AI employee. Try assigning it a complete task instead of asking whether it can perform one isolated function.

If you are developing an AI application, it is worth considering: why did the task originally need a person to open the software, learn a lot of operations, and complete it? Which information only needs to be explained to the Agent? Where does the person only need to make a final confirmation?

When the tool can handle the execution details on its own, humans can shift their attention back to the task, budget, quality, and judgment. This might be where agent-native tools truly become interesting.
